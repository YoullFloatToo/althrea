The sun slowly sank through the evening sky, and by the time Orin Dain stood up to climb down from the roof of the fisherman's shop, it had fallen low enough to rest upon the horizon, like a burning ember settling to the ground after a crack of smoldering wood.
 
An old man once told him to watch the sun set here, at the edge of the village along the port, where the ships launched out into the bay and glided out onto Faarlen. The location provided an unbroken view of the horizon and the waters extending out as far as the eye could see. Orin, not yet standing tall enough to see over the stacked barrels of salted fish, had added the part of climbing onto the roof.
 
Up on top of the fisherman's shop at the end of the port, with his legs hanging out over the edge of the roof, he could feel the cool mist of sea spray as the swells crashed against the rock wall below. He could smell the salt in the air, hear the hissing seafoam as the wake receded. He made a habit of coming here, sometimes not even to watch the sun set. He came simply to get away. Mostly by the dark of night, but sometimes by the light of day, although that was harder to pull off. It was Orin's spot, his escape from tough times.
 
From the vantage point Orin had, he could not only see the ocean extending out in front of him, but behind, he had a wide view of the whole of Greywharf, and the heart of the village sprawled out beneath him in the waning daylight.
 
A few fishmongers still lingered at the docks, finishing their work as they haggled over the last of the day’s catch, their voices rough from years spent shouting over the wind. Cawing gulls glided overhead, diving for scraps, though old Nelsa, the baker’s wife, stood guard near the fish stalls, swinging a broom at them with surprising vigor whenever they got too bold.
 
Further down the main street, the smith's forge glowed faintly from within, a chimney stack rising up out of the village like a spire, plumes of black smoke carried off by the wind. A broad-shouldered man with thick forearms finished up the last of the day's work, occasionally barking orders at the apprentice boy, barely a summer older than Orin, who lay dozing against a barrel.
 
Across from the forge, the Greywharf Inn and tavern were already alive with the sounds of laughter and raised voices as the evening crowd settled in for another of Mira's suppers. A few travelers had arrived earlier today, Orin had seen the wagons roll in, likely travelers heading south along the coast. The inn’s stable hand, a thin man with a permanent scowl, was tugging a stubborn mule toward the pens, cursing under his breath as it reared in protest.
 
Beyond the main street, lanterns flickered to life in open windows along the smaller alleys, casting long, wavering shadows. The scent of roasting fish drifted up out of the village, mingling with the ever-present salty air. Children ran barefoot between the houses, playing on their way back home for the night.
 
Everything in Greywharf looked the same as it always had, small, familiar, safe.
 
But Orin knew that was a lie.
 
He heard the way the adults had been whispering behind closed doors, saw the way the hunters had returned from the valley with their faces drawn tight, and recalled the many strained conversations he had with his father, who refused to entertain his curiosity.
 
Neither of them knew where he went during these times, when he disappeared, but his parents certainly wouldn’t approve if they did. On any other day, the setting sun would have meant the end of a day spent fishing from the docks, or a trek back in from the forest and through the village pastures. His mother would be expecting him soon. However, on this particular evening, sundown held another meaning for Orin. It meant that soon, the darkness would set, and the shadows would grow deep enough for him to disappear, slipping through the village streets, unseen.
 
What sparked Orin's interest, even more so than the village scenery or the setting sun, was what lie beyond, up in the foothills behind the village. The patch of dead trees in the forest leading up through the valley to the Vaesas was easily visible, like a bruise on the face of the land. It had been some weeks, and he meant to learn more, to finally find out exactly what was going on.
 
Feeling the warmth on his face, ablaze in the fading red of the setting sun, he made his way to the far end of the roof and dropped down. There was still some merchants and fishmongers out, closing their stalls. They were used to this, seeing Orin climbing down from Edric's shop. It was the same time he always did, and for now, he wouldn’t arouse any suspicion.
 
Making his way further down the main street, past the bakery and toward the smithy and inn. He could already see his destination, looming at the end of the winding main street. The village hall stood impressively at the edge of Greywharf. It was purposefully constructed on the elevated cliff edge of the bay’s rocky shore so that it would tower above the rest of the village, visible from any point. The front end completed with massive double doors able to swing open completely. They were meant as an invitation to all, so that any may be welcomed in voicing their troubles to the council. This is where his father would be holding tonight's meeting, and although the hall’s doors normally swept open, tonight, they were closed and bared. Tonight's was a meeting Orin was most certainly not invited to. Still, that had never stopped him before.
 
The music carried out through the windows of the inn as Orin crept past. It was the pride and joy of Greywharf, where one could count on a lively night. Locals and strangers, a feast, a bard, a warm fireplace burning beneath the hearth, the connected tavern pumping out buttered buns and pheasant pies. It was all exactly what one would need, and want, after a long journey along the coast, many nights spent making camp on the cold hard dirt.
 
And so it was always that, a remarkable life in the heart of Greywharf, bustling with visitors, travelers and locals alike, filling the village treasury with heaping pile of coin. Orin had spent plenty of night’s with his parents at the inn, enjoying a fine meal. But tonight, it was an obstacle.
 
He made his way along the edge of the Inn, ducking below windows, avoiding the revealing glow emanating from within. Some of the men and women who would be in tonight knew his parents, and they definitely would recognize the chief's, son. He made his way across the outside of the inn towards the back, his eyes set on his destination. It would have been plain to see during the day, a boy creeping along like a cat hunting a mouse, but under the cloak of night, Orin felt completely invisible.
 
"Up to no good, I see?" a woman's voice called out from the back of the inn, just as Orin was about to round the corner.
 
He froze mid-step, then stepped out knowing he'd already been caught.
 
The woman, swinging a bucket of slop into the waste pit, had her back turned to him, as if she didn’t need to look to know he was there.
 
Orin smirked, "Hi Mira, what’s tonight's supper?"
 
She emptied another bucket, its contents splattering thickly at the bottom of the pit.  
￼"Oh, just your favorite, roast hog with glazed honey and summer-root stuffing. Suppose they can’t all go right though," She said gesturing to the slop she just emptied into the pit.
 
Orin scrunched his face, "Ugh, really again? Boiled shoe leather tastes better."
 
Mira chuckled, wiping her hands on her apron, already soiled with stain. "Don’t tempt me, boy. You oughta eat more, put some meet on those bones."
 
Her face suddenly stiffened. "Now listen here, I know what you’re up to. It's been different around here lately. People here talkin' about strange happenings most every night. Things are going to change, at least for a while. You best drop these night prowls. You're not the wee shrimp you used to be."
 
"But I-"
 
She pointed a finger at him. "You promise me, tonight you head straight home. Or your mother will know. You hear?"
 
Orin looked up at Mira, although he was now almost as tall as she.
 
She gave him a testing look.
 
"I promise."
 
Mira nodded, satisfied with his response. "Alright now."
 
He took a step to leave, then hesitated, glancing back, "Mira, how did you know I was here?"
 
She picked up another bucket but didn't look at him. "Yeah, I saw you up on Edric's shop again."
 
She laughed as she dumped the bucket. "Stand out against the sky like a seagull dropping a shite mid-flight. Couldn’t be more visible, even from here. Figured you’d be sneakin' off this way, knowing what’s goin' on up in the hall tonight."
 
Orin giggled at that. She was always one step ahead of him.
 
"Good night, Mira."
 
"See ye in the mornin'," she said with a wave as she stepped through the back door into the tavern.
 
Smiling, Orin continued down the main street.
 
As he walked, he thought about what Mira had said. _Why had she been so serious?_ Mira always had a way of getting him and the other children to follow the rules, even the most rebellious. She related to them, earned their respect, somehow made them want to do it, for her sake. But it was never so direct. Orin thought back to the way she looked at him. _She sounded like mother,_ he thought. Something about the interaction made Orin’s mind spiral.
 
He stopped just before an intersection in the main street. The path he took would decide which he honored more. His word, or his curiosity.
 
Orin thought back to everything that happened over the previous weeks, since that evening on the fisherman’s shop. It was like any other. Sneaking past the stalls and climbing up the barrels, hanging his feet over the edge, the sun setting over the ocean, the view of the village. But he had stayed longer that evening, not leaving till well past the sun set. And when he stood up to go, lost in contemplation of the scolding he’d get for staying out too late, he saw it.
 
It was the largest bird he had ever laid his eyes on. Entirely black. Barely visible in the dwindling twilight, but it was there in the distance.
 
It rose up from the forest deep within the valley between the foothills. His eyes tracked it as it continued to rise higher above the tree tops. Its giant wings pumping with strength as they grasped at the air, pulling it upwards into the sky before disappearing into the bottom of the grey cover of clouds. Orin squinted. What had he just seen? He looked around for anyone else who might have noticed it. He was alone. The entire experience had been little more than a glimpse, but the image of it remained burned into his mind's eye, even weeks later, the scene replaying in his head on sleepless nights. It was just too odd. It happened too fast. Its movement, unnatural. And those wings, although too far to be sure, looked featherless.
 
As Orin stood there at the intersection, his father’s voice echoed in his head.
 
“That is quite far enough.” were the last words said when Orin tried to bring the topic up for the last time.
 
The weeks that followed the sighting brought a stillness over Greywharf. Whispered conversation became the norm, in the streets, in the alleys. The voices, abruptly stopping if Orin showed himself, and he was turned down when he tried to ask for more. Yet he still heard some of it, mostly from the other children. They said it was a fire, that two had been found dead. Orin didn’t believe it, not from them. It didn’t make sense, and they hadn't seen what he had.
 
Orin looked to the left, down the street towards his home, his mother was probably already fixed to the door, wooden spoon in hand, waiting for him to return. Then he looked to the right, up to the village hall. The warm glow radiating from the windows.
 
_That is quite far enough,_ his father’s voice echoed in his head again.
 
Orin turned to the right and started toward the village hall. He had to know more.
 
_Sorry, Mira._
 
And so there Orin was, again, pressing himself against the cold, stone back wall of the village hall. The wind in his hair, the waves crashing below, the moonlight guiding his way. His movements were practiced, yet careful. He had done this before. Up the old wooden fence post, onto the lower portion of the roof and up to the back wall. He used the same hand holds, pressed his feet against the same wooden supports, now showing wear from his many climbs, slipping through the second-floor window when his father worked late, stealing glances at maps and letters he couldn't yet read.
 
Peering through the rafters, heart hammering in his chest, he saw them gathered at the long table. His father and chief of Greywharf, Garrick Dain, was seated at the head.
 
The voices below were already thick with argument.
 
A man's voice called out**.** "Madness. That’s what this is. Every few years, something like this happens. A storm fells trees in the hills, and suddenly, the old wives start whispering of shadows in the night sky. A child goes missing, and it's not wolves. No, it's a winged beast**.** And now? A patch of dead forest, and we're to believe some phantom sucked the life straight out of it?"
 
Another voice, slow and steady. "I saw it with my own eyes, Veyne. Those trees weren't burned. No scorch marks, no blackened charcoal. The bark was brittle, like old paper. Turned to ash in my hands. Tell me, does that sound like wildfire to you?"
 
"Joren, you-"
 
A woman's voice interjected. "What he speaks of is true. Fire doesn't wound a forest; it spreads with the wind, consuming it whole. We would have seen it raging up in the hills for days, or even weeks. And then it would have been on us."
 
Veyne sneered. "And what of disease, Marla? Have you considered that? A sickness in the earth, a rot that spread through the roots? Perhaps, a spread slow enough to escape our notice till now."
 
"I hadn't considered that possibility."
 
"And yet you sit there so certain, as if you know the roots of the world. You act above us all, yet fail to-"
 
"I hadn't considered that possibility," Marla cut in, her voice sharper this time. "But I do not believe it to be the case. Disease spreads gradually, without strict borders**.** This… It happened all at once. You can see it from here, a perfectly contained space."
 
Another man had been silent until now. Orin recognized his voice, a man named Halloway who often visited his father late at night, their voices always hushed in discussion.
 
Halloway exhaled through his nose. "I rode out to see it myself this morning. It's not just the trees and undergrowth."
 
He directed his attention to the head of the table. Garrick met his gaze.
 
"It's the wildlife too. The birds." Halloway’s voice was low and measured, but there was something uneasy in it. "Their corpses are piled around the bases of the trees, like dead leaves in autumn. And there was more. A deer, a fox…"
 
He trailed off, eyes distant, as if reliving the moment.
 
He lifted his head and spoke again, his voice grim. "What unsettled me most, I could not detect even a hint of the stench of death. It’s been weeks, and yet the corpses littering the ground remain as if they had fallen mere moments ago. No sign of scavengers picking for scrap meat, no rot, nothing. The forest there is entirely lifeless, and frozen."
 
For a moment, none spoke.
 
"And what of the children?"
 
A heavy silence followed, the words hanging in the air. The voice was calm, yet it demanded respect. It cut through the tension like a blade through flesh.
 
Orin's father spoke again, "Perhaps all of you have forgotten what brought us here. The reason we sit around this table."
 
He straightened, his tone rising. "The reason we bicker of wildfire and disease."
 
The chair scrapped against the floorboards as Garrick stood up throwing it backward, the sound cracking through the hall like a thunderclap.
 
He pointed to the front of the hall erupting with anger, his voice reverberating through the room. "The reason those doors are sealed shut, when never under my command have they been before!"
 
The room tensed. The others shifted with unease in their seats.
 
But Garrick did not stop.
 
"We sup on Edric's provisions while he goes home to an empty house! And yet, none of you say it outright. You dance around the word as if speaking its name will summon it into our midst."
 
The sudden shock of his father's voice caused Orin to jump. The wooden beam creaked, a plume of dust fell below.
 
They all looked up.
 
Orin ducked back into cover, bumping his head on the ceiling.
 
Veyne stood, his eyes squinting.
 
"Who's there?" he called.
 
In the silence, Orin's face winced, his head ached and his body shivered, but he remained still. He dared not make another sound.
 
Orin wanted to make for the window, to escape, to flee back to his house and welcome the whipping his mother surely had ready for him. But he was too afraid to move, frozen on the spot. His mind raced and his heart thudded against his chest like a galloping horses hooves striking the earth. _What do I do?_
 
But before he could make a move, his father broke the silence.
 
"No one is there, Veyne."
 
Orin let out a silent sigh of relief, and then peeked his head back out just in time to see his father leaning on the table, hands spread wide, his gaze turning to each of the others.
 
Joren's eyes met his. "We are afraid, Garrick."
 
Veyne scoffed as he sat back down. "Afraid of bedtime stories, more like."
 
Joren, hands clenched. "My grandfather saw one. The year the river poisoned the fish and their carcasses collected on the banks for miles. A girl vanished in the night. He swore to his dying breath of what he saw amongst the clouds."
 
Orin squinted. _It was in the clouds._
 
Veyne shook his head. "And your grandfather was a drunken old fool."
 
The table erupted.
 
"Watch your tongue, Veyne!" Joren growled.
 
"No!" Garrick’s voice cut through the clamor like a blade.
 
Silence fell.
 
Garrick leaned forward, sighing. "We don’t know the truth in what we’re dealing with. We have accounts from people I trust. People with reason, non-superstitious. They say the death in the forest is draethblight."
 
He remained silent for a moment, thinking of the children.
 
"We have others who dismiss it as nonsense." He turned to Veyne. "If we do nothing, and it really is what some claim it to be, what then? Will you sit in that fine house of yours and pray the beast does not hunger for more?"
 
Veyne shifted uncomfortably but said nothing.￼  
Halloway leaned forward. "We cannot afford inaction. If there’s even a chance, a sliver of a chance, that a draeth lurks in those hills, we need to protect the village. We need to know."
 
"We'll send people who are willing to find out the truth," Garrick offered.
 
Marla nodded. "Yes, but if we wait too long, we may not have the luxury of sending hunters. It may come to us instead."
 
Veyne grumbled. "And you think wasting coin on some ragged sellswords will solve it?"
 
Garrick’s gaze was sharp. "Coin is a small price to pay for certainty."
 
A pause. Then, he continued.
 
"We'll post a bounty. Word will spread. Mercenaries, warriors, and fools alike will come. Whether the beast is real or not, the valley must be searched. At worst, we put a few gold coins into the hands of desperate men. At best, we'll know the truth, and maybe even kill whatever is lurking in that valley before it comes down upon us."
 
Veyne threw up his hands. "Why are you so intent on turning Greywharf into a circus? The roads will be crawling with bounty hunters looking for a monster that doesn’t exist."
 
Garrick’s expression was hard as stone. "Let them come. If nothing else, their presence will keep the village from falling into panic. People find comfort in knowing action is being taken."
 
Veyne fell silent, then spoke in a defeated tone, "I do not see the logic in this, and yet I know the decision has already been made. I will support you regardless."
 
Joren rubbed his chin. "How do we get the word out? We need more than the local gossip to carry it."
 
Halloway nodded. "We’ll send riders to the next few towns, post notices in the trading outposts along the coast. Maybe even send word with one of the merchant caravans passing through."
 
Garrick added, "Have the innkeepers spread the news. Greywharf is a resting point for many travelers. They’ll take the tale further than we ever could."
 
Marla hesitated. "And what if this brings too many on us? What if the wrong kind come?"
 
Garrick exhaled. "Then we defend our own should it come to that. A draeth, we might not be prepared for. But people. We can do that."
 
He looked around the room. "The bounty will be posted by sundown, tomorrow. Halloway, you will oversee it."
 
Halloway nodded.
 
“And include a request for any who have knowledge of draethlore. That may bring even more fools upon us, but we may be able to sort the legitimates from the frauds, and perhaps learn the truth before even sending anyone up there.”
 
“Of course.” Halloway concluded.
 
Garrick stood. "This meeting is adjourned. Pray that we are wrong."
 
As the room emptied, the tension lingered in the air. And with it, Garrick. He remained fixed to the head of the table, still leaning on it with both hands, head hung low. Even after the sounds of the others faded and the hall's large doors clapped shut, he stood in the silence like that for some time.
 
In the commotion, Orin ducked back into cover and sat still. He didn't move, too afraid to make another sound. He couldn’t let his father know he was there, but he needed to get out and back home first. Still, he waited for his father to move, hoping to use the sound to mask his own.
 
And then, in the silence, he heard more.
 
"You were almost seen, you fool. Show yourself." Garrick's voice echoed from below.
 
A bolt of fear rippled up from Orin's gut, catching his breath in his throat. _How?_ He thought. What had given him away? Defeated, he made to call out to his father, but another voice cut through the air instead.
 
"I'm afraid I have no idea what you are talking about, Garrick Dain."
 
The man's voice came like thin rasps, Garrick's name spat out with venom.
 
Orin froze. He wanted to see who was down below, but he didn't dare move_. Had one of the others returned?_ Then his father spoke again, his voice wavering.
 
"I thought you were up-"
 
"It matters not what you thought. I was told you could handle the rest of the council," the voice hissed. "That sneering one, Veyne is it? He is suspicious. We'll have to deal with that one. We shall see how many the bounty will turn up."
 
"You will not lay a hand on Veyne. I've done all that you've asked. All is accounted for," Garrick spit, voice laced with disgust.
 
"Not all, Dain. As I recall, you agreed to provide your city guard as well, should too few care to attend. You saw for yourself even some of your appointed here tonight hardly believe in the matter."
 
Orin heard his father take a step closer to the man. "Do you honestly expect that I would leave my people undefended? And for how long? There's no telling how long your little… experiment is going to take."
 
"Oh, don’t fool yourself, Dain. The patrolmen in your employ are so lax they spend more time defending bottles of booze smuggled to their post than they do the people. My men have slipped into place without issue. Of course that’s understandable, this being the Vaestradi slumlands.”
 
"You scum," Garrick's voice grumbled from below.
 
The other man continued, his voice smugly lacking care for the insult. "The defense of the village is simply none of my concern. When I first came to you on that night, seems so long ago now doesn't it? Hmm can you remind me, was that before or after the night those children ran off?"
 
A moment of silence passed. Orin heard his father say nothing.
 
"Ahh yes, I remember now. Pity, those two. I informed you of your new guest and offered a way to be rid of it. But you hesitated, and now we're here. Well, it seems your side of the deal was subject to change. As is mine. You will find a way to ensure that bounty of yours will attract enough, and I will not reveal the leader of Greywharf to be a man of secrecy and inaction."
 
"That wasn't our arrangement," Garrick said through gritted teeth.
 
Suddenly, Orin heard footsteps.
 
And then, the whine of chairs scrapping the floor. Wood cracked and clanged to the floor. Thuds and grunting voices echoed up from below as a struggle broke out.
 
Then a hollow slam pierced Orin's ears, shaking him to action.
 
He scurried back to the edge of the rafters and looked down below. Shock and fear washed over him as he saw his father pinned to the table, his face bloodied and pressed up against the wood. Two hooded men held him down on either side, one threating to snap his arm at the elbow. A third figure stood off to the side, hands behind his back.
 
"Now, our arrangement is that you do as I say, and your wife and son continue to walk. Unlike you, my word is absolute."
 
The world had suddenly grown into a place far greater than the safe and familiar confines of Greywharf, and for the first time in his life, sitting up in the rafters of the village hall with his hands pressed over his mouth and tears streaming down his cheeks, Orin found himself wishing he had obeyed his mother.
