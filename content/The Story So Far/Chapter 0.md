Plenty have discussed while plenty have cried, and plenty more screamed and argued in a mead burning rage or sober as a bird, with their family, their friends, or strangers alike, that what happened up in those hills resulting in that black spot of death was nothing but a case of disease, wildfire, or something far more sinister.
 
Of course, regardless of what plenty have said, whether it were a truth, a falsehood, some combination of both, or a complete fabrication of their own minds, there was only one way about it, which they would soon come to know, and it started with an old man chasing after a pair of young children through the village pasters and out to the forest just beyond.
 
---
 
"Slow down, you two!" the old man called after them.
 
The children, of course, ignored him, as children do. They skipped ahead with a devious excitement, knowing they could easily outpace a slow old man, leaving him in the dust in their wake. The old man knew what they were thinking, and so he smiled and shook his head, hurrying his pace to catch up.
 
The father of those young children, entrusting his two little ones to the old man, had embarked on another week’s sail over the Faarlen waters. He was a fisherman. He was The Fisherman, in Greywharf, where folks often referred to him as The King of Faarlen, because of his uncanny ability to draw fish from the ocean. Old Niero, the man who spent quite a few of his elder years training the fisherman, of course before the problem with his hip came about, would swear to you there were some arcane workings behind the nature of it, that those fish had half a mind to be caught, and willingly leaped from the water into that boat so he could sail them ashore, but that was Old Niero.
 
The rest of the townsfolk believed in the fisherman as simply a skilled man who knew how to read the waters. For this reason the old man with which this story begins, felt almost in debt to the fisherman, for he often spent evenings at the Inn, dining on a fresh catch.
 
After the passing of their mother, the old man had offered his care for the children, thinking it would help offset the fisherman's efforts for the village, and take a load off a grieving man. The arrangement soon became a regular thing. Someone with a greater ambition, or perhaps just younger, would have sought the opportunity with a mind for additional compensation, especially for such frequent requests. After all, Lucan and Alara were more than a day's work, but the old man, being as near the end of his life as he'd ever been, and having already chased his own ambitions in his youth, although missing the opportunity to father any of his own, had reached a point in his life where he found that he did not mind the chaotic doings of children. In fact, he secretly cherished them.
 
These were the days when he got to spend his time dancing in the rain, or chasing glittering June bugs. On these days, the sun shone brighter, the Inn's tarts tasted sweeter, and the birdsong sounded as wondrous as a band of bards. On these days, he too, was a child. Awash in the nostalgia of his youth, he smiled at the grazing cattle as he passed them by.
 
They were headed out to the nearby woods, just beyond the village borders. Here, the trees grew comfortably, with plenty of space to stretch their branches. But further in, their number increased, and the forest grew dense and shaded. The woods swept up through the valley between the foothills, carrying through the winding paths farther than anyone knew. As the sun sinks through the evening, the foothills cast the valley into a deep shadow, and the forest sees night far before the sun sets.
 
Lucan and Alara made a habit of running out to the forest most mornings, which the old man allowed. However, on this occasion, Alara had been feeling ill, and they spent most of the day lounging about the old man's home. Despite his own boyhood, a child's energy still amazed the old man. Even when faced with illness, they won't be down for long.
 
"But why not? I feel fine!" Alara cried as she tugged on the old man's robes.
 
"You mustn't tire yourself further. I always say, a day's rest and a full belly makes you strong n' blessed and maybe a bit smelly HA HA!"
 
They all laughed together. And then Lucan grumbled.
 
"She wasn't really ill. She just didn't want to help in the garden."
 
"Yes I was!"
 
"No you weren't. If you were, then your hair would stand up on your head like a rooster's comb. That's what mother used to say."
 
"Don't talk about mother!" the girl cried.
 
Before things got out of hand, the old man jumped in, "Alright little ones settle down. It's okay, if you must visit the forest today, so be it. But you'll not be out long. The dark settles in the forest before sunset, you'll be out by then. Oh, and I'm coming with," the old man said, laughing.
 
"Race you there!"
 
And just like that, they were off without another word.
 
As the old man drew near the forest's edge, his tired eyes scanned through the trees for the children. They had outrun him long before he made it out to the pasture gates, and vanished into the woods. He cursed his old age. His eyes could not see as they used to. Even so, he made his way through the tree line, knowing where they would be headed.  
A stream flowed down through the foothill valley from the Vaesas to the east, carving a path through the forest. At the bottom, a babbling brook bubbled and splashed. The little ones usually played there, building dams, catching frogs, and jumping across the rocks that lay in the flowing water. He remembered, as a boy, he visited the very location himself. _That was just a moon ago,_ he thought. Chuckling to himself, he continued on.
 
The old man new something was wrong before he even arrived at the brook. He was old, but he could still see. The children were not there. Concerned, he made his way up to the brook and looked around, thinking they might be nearby.
 
"You two!" he called out to them.
 
He listened for a response, and although his hearing wasn't perfect, he knew he would be able to hear them should they call back, even over the babbling waters. _Of course, they are hiding._ He started over to a dense patch of trees near the brook, thinking they would be waiting behind them, ready to jump out at him. As he made his way across the stepping stones the little ones had laid in the water some weeks before, walking as nimbly as his stubborn legs could, he noticed the footprints. Small footprints. They certainly had been here. The old man squinted as his eyes traced along the tracks. _Curious,_ he thought. The footprints didn't lead over to the dense patch of trees, as he assumed. Instead, they followed the stream, leading up towards the valley, and into the woods that would soon grow dark. Nervously, he trotted along, following the two sets of footprints deeper into the forest and into the valley.
 
The old man, although lenient in many ways most elders were not, was hard as steel in the rules he did set. He had forbid the children from playing any further into the woods than the brook. The old man spoke to them with the sternness of a man much larger and stronger than himself. Making it known to the children that if they disobeyed, he would be very angry. Of course, that was what he wanted them to think. And it worked. Something about the idea of the old man, normally sweet and joyous, always with a smile on his face, showing even a hint of anger, was enough to keep the children in line. At least, until today, it seemed. As the old man glanced up through the rustling leaves of the forest canopy and watched the sun dip below the crest of the foothills, enveloping himself and the surrounding trees in darkness, it was not anger that he felt. It was fear.
 
The old man broke into an anxious trot, willing his weakened legs to move faster than they had in a long time. He thought back to his younger days, when the lean muscle fanned across his body like a stone mosaic. When he could run for leagues, easily swing a chopped log over his shoulder, and scarf down an entire pheasant in one sitting. His charm had the village lassies turning their heads. Of course, he cared only for one. He had spent decades with her. Years of quiet living and the ironclad passage of time had melted the muscle from his body, hunched his back, and buried his wife. _Why think of that at a time like this?_ He thought as he hastened his pace even further.
 
By the time the old man slowed to a stop, his breath came in ragged gasps and wheezes. His knees trembled beneath his weight and he feared he could not go on. Depleted, he braced himself against a nearby tree, willing his eyes to adjust to the gloom. It had been some time since he started to follow the tracks of small feet, and he was now deep within the valley and the shaded region of the forest. Although it was dark, the sun had not yet dipped below the horizon, and he could still see well enough to make out the tracks. Or rather, where they should have been had they not suddenly discontinued.
 
The forest here was silent. Perhaps too silent. The old man heard not the rusting of leaves, nor the buzzing of insects, not even the familiar birdsong which so often enveloped the forest. Only the gentle flow of the stream and his own rasping breaths. He stood there for a time, eventually settling his breathing into a slow rhythmic pulse, but his heart still pounded within his chest like a scared animal trying to break free from its cage. He scanned his surroundings, searching for clues. _Oh gods, let me find them,_ he thought to himself.
 
Something brushed the back of his neck, sending a shiver down his spine and a rush of adrenalin through his blood. He half jumped, half spun on his heel, turning with all the dexterity an old man possesses. A crunch beneath his foot as he stepped. The old man glanced downward, lifting his foot to see flakes of crushed dead leaves. Glancing upwards at the treetop, he saw a canopy covered with the dry oranges and browns of autumn. Even as he looked, another dead leaf fell before him with none of summer’s grace, landing at his feet. He picked it up, inspecting it as it crumbled between his forefinger and thumb. _Dead in the prime of summer?_ He questioned. Reaching out, he felt the trunk. The ashy grey bark splintered and cracked at his touch. _Dry rot?_  And as he looked passed the dead trunk out into the rest of the forest, he saw there was more.
 
Withered undergrowth littered the ground, bundles of twisted stems rising up grom the ground like dead hands, their fingers curling inwards as if grasping at his legs. He reached for one, a fern that should have been lush, vibrant, teeming with life, yet as his fingers brushed against it, the leaf crumbled into dust, the particles floating off in the wind. A cold weight settled in his stomach. He turned his gaze up stream, and only then did he notice, the trees ahead stood as skeletal husks, their bark split and peeling, as if death was but an icy wind that swept the valley.
 
The old man moved deeper into the valley, traversing the unnatural decay. He soon found himself amidst a grey labyrinth of twisting twigs and branches, all color drained from sight. The leaves came barreling down around him, the curled bark peeled off trunks falling to the ground, undergrowth dissolved to dust underneath his feet. His every move crushed, crunched, and cracked. Dust filled the air. His eyes stung and burned, tears welled up blurring his vision. He broke into a fit of cough. Even as he pulled the collar of his robes over his mouth and nose, his breath came in strained wheezes. All at the same time, anxiety, guilt, and fear welled up inside him like rushing rapids, threatening to break his resolve. But over that, a raging storm swirled, his will to protect them, to find the children. He had to find the children.
 
Despite his age, the old man pressed on with the strength of his youth. He desperately called out to them.
 
“Alaraaa! Lucaaan!"
 
There was no response.
 
He called again, lifting his hands to his mouth to direct his voice, but a protruding dead root caught his foot, sending him crashing to the ground. He reached his hands out to brace his fall. As he caught himself, his wrist bent backwards at an unnatural angle. Heat shot up through his forearm like a bolt of lightning. A moan escaped his lips as he pulled his hand in close to his chest and rolled over in pain. He held up his wrist, examining it, his hand drooping to the side in a way it shouldn’t. It was broken. He looked up, willing his eyes to locate any sign of the children, but he felt a child himself as the pain drew tears from his eyes. He bolstered his resolve, standing up and wiping the tears away.
 
He scanned his surroundings. Somehow, he had made his way deeper into the forest and away from the stream. Had he gotten turned around? Searching every direction, he looked for something familiar amongst the grey ashy desolation. A tree he remembered passing, a ditch he remembered crossing, an outcropping of rock he circled around. Maybe his own tracks? Then he thought, _Their tracks. They followed the stream._ Closing his eyes, he listened. He couldn’t have gone far. He should have been able to hear the gentle flow of water running through the forest, revealing which direction to go. But there was nothing, total silence. Not even the wind blew here.
 
He opened his eyes.
 
And then, in the stillness, he saw them.
 
_No. No, that can’t be right._
 
He blinked, his vision blurry from dust and tears. He tried to refocus. But the shapes remained, small and still, unnaturally still. His breath hitched in his throat.
 
He stepped forward, then stopped.
 
_They’re just sleeping, he told himself. Resting, like children do after playing too hard._
 
But children did not sleep like this. Not with their hands limp at their sides, their chests unmoving, their heads hanging crooked from the slack in their neck.
 
The old man’s fingers twitched. _Reach for them,_ his mind screamed. _Touch them. Wake them._
 
But his body refused.
 
Instead, his eyes drifted, picking apart the scene one terrible detail at a time.
 
The two children lay slumped beside each other, hands nearly touching, faces frozen in emotionless expression. Their skin was ashen, their lips pale, their wide, unblinking eyes staring at nothing at all.
 
The strength drained from his legs. He collapsed to his knees, barely feeling the pain in his broken wrist as he gasped for breath.
 
A ragged sob clawed at his throat, but no sound came. There was nothing left in the world but this silent, lifeless place. He did not move. He simply stared ahead; his gaze fixed on the two motionless corpses in disbelief. And then, as he knelt there among the blackened forest, a wave of memory crashed over him carrying voices from across a lifetime.
 
He had heard the words as a wide-eyed boy at the feet of his elders, as a youth during nights by the campfire, as a young man in the tales of wandering travelers bathed in the dim glow of taverns and inns, and whispered by the delirious old as he himself grew gray with age. The stories were always the same: a shadow streaking through the sky, wings like the sails of a galleon, talons and teeth like steel daggers, death, and blight.
 
Staring at the withered ruin before him, he now understood. He knew what had done this, what had drained the life from the trees, the birds, the forest itself, and the children who lie dead upon the earth.
 
“Draeth.” the old man whispered.
 
As he lay there, motionless in the ashen wastes, his eyes fixed on the lifeless bodies of the children he had failed to protect, he heard it.
 
Slow. Rhythmic. Like the pulse of a coming storm.
 
Deep, thunderous beats of enormous wings boomed in the distance. And then a great gust tore through the hollowed forest, snapping brittle branches and spreading dust across the valley.
